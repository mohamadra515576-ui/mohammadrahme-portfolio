01 SaaS Blog Post

Reducing churn through onboarding

How a product-led team rebuilt their first-week experience to cut 30-day churn by a third. Includes the email mistakes that quietly compound into retention problems.

Format

Outcome

5 onboarding email mistakes that are quietly increasing your churn

Most SaaS companies treat onboarding emails as an afterthought — a welcome message, maybe a feature tour, then silence until the renewal reminder. But onboarding emails are often the single biggest lever a SaaS company has over early churn, and small mistakes here compound fast.

  1. Leading with features instead of outcomes. New users don't care that your product "has 40 integrations." They care whether it solves the specific problem that made them sign up. The first email should reference that problem directly, not list capabilities.
  2. Sending the same sequence to every user type. A solo freelancer and a 50-person marketing team are not onboarding for the same reason. If your product serves multiple use cases, your onboarding emails should branch based on the signup answers or first actions the user took.
  3. Waiting too long to define "activation." If you don't know what a genuinely activated user looks like — the specific action that correlates with retention — your onboarding emails are guessing. Find that action first, then build every email toward getting the user there.
  4. No clear next step in each email. Every onboarding email should have exactly one call to action. Multiple links or asks dilute attention and reduce the odds any single one gets clicked.
  5. Treating onboarding as a fixed sequence, not a loop. Users who stall at step 3 need a different email than users who sailed through it. Static sequences miss this. Behavior-triggered follow-ups catch users before they go quiet.

The companies that reduce early churn aren't necessarily the ones with the most polished product — they're the ones who treat the first two weeks of the user relationship as seriously as the sales process that got the user there in the first place.

02 Case Study Framework

A case study that actually closes deals

A repeatable structure for turning customer wins into a sales asset that actually closes deals — used across more than 30 published case studies to date.

For
In-house content team
Format
Internal framework + template
Outcome
Sales adopted it as the default

Structure I use for every SaaS case study:

  • The hook — one sentence with the headline result (e.g., "How [Company] cut onboarding time by 40% in six weeks")
  • The situation — what the customer's world looked like before, in their own language, not generic pain points
  • Why they chose [Product] — the specific moment or reason, not a generic feature comparison
  • The implementation — what actually happened, kept concrete and short
  • The result — specific numbers where possible, framed around what the reader cares about
  • A direct quote — even one strong sentence from the customer does more than three paragraphs of narration
  • Close — soft CTA relevant to where this case study will live (blog, sales deck, etc.)

Most SaaS blog content fails for one reason: it's written by someone who doesn't understand the product.

I spend the first hour of any project just reading the docs, the changelog, and the support forum — before I write a single word.

It's slower. It's also the only way the content doesn't sound generic.

Post 2 — value / mini-lesson

3 questions I ask before writing any SaaS blog post:

  • What does the reader believe right now that's wrong?
  • What's the one thing they need to understand to trust this product?
  • What page do I want them to click to next?

If a draft doesn't answer these, it's not ready — no matter how well it's written.
